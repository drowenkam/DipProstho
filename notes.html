Source bridge · F Pros Chapter 1

DIPLOMA LEARNING OUTCOMES 1.1, 1.5, 1.11 · 2a.5–2a.6

CORE PRINCIPLE

Translate anatomy into risk, denture extension and examination findings—not an isolated list of muscle names.

01 Functional anatomy at the chairside

Map the muscles and frena to the denture borders: lip and cheek muscles shape the polished surface; the mylohyoid, retromylohyoid area and tongue control lower lingual extension. An apparently retentive denture may still be unstable if its contours conflict with muscle movement.

02 Structures that alter the plan

In an atrophic mandible, consider the proximity of the mental nerve to the denture-bearing surface. In the maxilla, examine palatal form, tuberosities and soft-palate movement before deciding the posterior palatal seal or a palatal connector.

03 Risk and referral

Anatomical complexity becomes clinically relevant when it changes radiographic selection, impression design or safety. Spreading infection with airway concern, complex soft-tissue pathology or unexplained neuropathy requires escalation; detailed surgery is outside this Diploma supplement.

04 One-best-answer cue

A short mandibular lingual flange may lose the seal; excessive extension can dislodge the denture during tongue action. Diagnose the functional border before changing the occlusion or adding adhesive.

SCOPE / GUIDANCE

Level 2: recognition, examination, prosthesis design and timely referral. No specialist anatomical surgery is implied.

CLINICAL DISCRIMINATOR

The decisive distinction is movement-related displacement versus inadequate static adaptation. A new denture may pass a finger-pull test yet dislodge whenever the patient smiles. Observe the causal movement and use pressure indicating paste before removing acrylic.

CASE CONTRAST / NEAR MISS

A lower denture is stable at rest but rises during protrusion of the tongue. Border extension and polished-surface form are the first checks; increasing vertical dimension or providing an adhesive is less defensible.

Physiology and pathology: clinical foundations

Exam orientation: Frequency of sugar and acid, rather than total quantity alone, is important in caries and erosion. Saliva contributes to denture retention and protects natural teeth.

Critical pHEnamel ~5.5; root dentine/cementum ~6.2–6.7; fluorapatite ~4.5.

Oral lesionsPersistent unexplained ulceration and suspicious red or red-and-white lesions require urgent assessment.

2.1 Saliva

Volume and flow: Approximately 0.5–1.5 L/day. Unstimulated flow is about 0.3–0.4 mL/min. Hyposalivation is commonly defined as unstimulated flow below 0.1 mL/min or stimulated flow below approximately 0.5–0.7 mL/min.

Functions: Lubrication, buffering (bicarbonate is a main buffer in stimulated saliva), remineralisation, antimicrobial protection (lysozyme, lactoferrin, IgA and histatins), taste, bolus formation and denture retention.

Denture retention: A thin saliva film enables adhesion (saliva to the denture and mucosa), cohesion (within saliva), interfacial surface tension, viscosity and atmospheric pressure acting with the peripheral seal. Thick, ropy saliva may impair retention.

Common causes of dry mouth: Antidepressants, antihistamines, antimuscarinics, diuretics, antipsychotics, Sjögren's syndrome, head and neck radiotherapy, poorly controlled diabetes, dehydration, anxiety and mouth breathing.

Management: Identify and treat the cause; consider sugar-free stimulants and saliva substitutes, and prevention with appropriate fluoride. Selection of systemic sialogogues such as pilocarpine requires assessment of contraindications and supervision.

2.2 Mastication and neuromuscular control

Periodontal mechanoreceptors provide fine tactile and load feedback. Complete-denture wearers instead rely more on mucosal and temporomandibular-joint receptors. Implants provide osseoperception, although feedback is less sensitive than that from a natural tooth.

Complete-denture wearers achieve roughly one-fifth to one-sixth of the bite force and masticatory performance of dentate individuals (source estimate). A shortened dental arch with anterior teeth and premolars (about 3–5 occlusal units) may provide adequate function for some older adults; a distal-extension RPD does not automatically improve function. The source cites the WHO aim of retaining at least 20 functional teeth throughout life.

Ageing, Parkinson's disease, dementia and stroke may reduce adaptation to a new prosthesis. A copy-denture technique may be appropriate when preserving familiar features improves tolerance.

2.3 Consequences of tooth loss

Resorption pattern: The maxilla resorbs upwards and inwards, making the upper arch smaller; the mandible resorbs downwards and outwards, making it wider. Long-standing edentulism can lead to an apparent Class III jaw relationship and a posterior crossbite.

Local effects: Tooth drifting, tipping, over-eruption, loss of interocclusal space, changes in occlusal vertical dimension, and loss of lip or cheek support. Ridge resorption is often most marked during the first 6–12 months after extraction and continues more slowly thereafter. The source cites Tallgren’s long-term observation of approximately fourfold greater anterior mandibular height loss than anterior maxillary height loss.

2.3 Consequences of tooth loss — continued

Resorption is influenced by anatomical and metabolic factors (bone volume, density, age), function and parafunction, and prosthetic factors such as denture fit and continuous wear. Residual roots, overdenture abutments and implants may help preserve ridge form in suitable cases; the clinical choice depends on prognosis, maintenance and patient goals.

2.4 Caries

Critical pH: Enamel approximately 5.5; root dentine and cementum approximately 6.2–6.7; fluorapatite approximately 4.5. On the Stephan curve, plaque pH falls rapidly after sugar intake and then gradually recovers; frequent exposure increases risk.

Microbiology and ecology: Streptococcus mutans is associated with initiation, lactobacilli with lesion progression, and Actinomyces species with root caries. The ecological plaque hypothesis emphasises frequent sugar and low salivary flow as environmental selectors.

Enamel lesion zones: Translucent zone, dark zone, body of lesion and relatively intact surface zone. In dentine, infected, heavily contaminated tissue must be distinguished from affected, potentially remineralisable tissue when planning selective removal.

Root caries risk: Gingival recession, hyposalivation, removable-prosthesis plaque retention, reduced dexterity and frequent dietary sugar.

Prevention: Tailor brushing, fluoridated toothpaste, fluoride varnish, sugar-frequency advice, cleaning around clasps and denture saddles, and dry-mouth management to the patient's risk. The source notebook cites Delivering Better Oral Health (2021), including 1,450 ppm fluoride for routine adult brushing, higher-fluoride preparations for prescribed risk groups and 22,600 ppm fluoride varnish. Confirm current age-based prescribing guidance before clinical use.

2.7 Potentially malignant disorders and oral cancer

Suspicious lesions: Erythroplakia carries substantial malignant risk. Non-homogeneous leukoplakia, especially at the lateral tongue and floor of mouth, warrants careful assessment. These sites are also important for oral squamous-cell carcinoma.

Risk factors: Tobacco, alcohol (with synergistic risk), areca nut, immunosuppression, a history of oral cancer, and site-specific HPV associations.

Referral: The source notebook cites NICE NG12: unexplained oral ulceration lasting more than three weeks; persistent oral or lip lumps; and suspicious red or red-and-white patches warrant an appropriate urgent suspected-cancer pathway. Do not repeatedly adjust a denture in place of referring a persistent unexplained lesion.

2.8 Ageing and systemic links

Ageing teeth: Secondary dentine deposition, smaller pulp chambers, potentially reduced sensibility responses, increased brittleness and darker shade.

Haematinic deficiency: Iron, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency may present with glossitis, angular cheilitis, recurrent ulceration or burning symptoms.

Diabetes: Associated with periodontal disease, candidosis, xerostomia and potentially impaired healing.

Osteoporosis: Osteoporosis alone is not an automatic contraindication to implants. Antiresorptive medication exposure and individual MRONJ risk require separate assessment.

Examiner discriminators

  • Root dentine demineralises at a higher pH than enamel.
  • Newton type III denture stomatitis refers to papillary hyperplasia.
  • Miconazole can interact with warfarin, even with oral topical formulations.
  • Restorations standing proud of eroded tooth tissue suggest erosion rather than attrition alone.

03 Denture-specific prevention

Take dentures out overnight and clean them with non-abrasive products. Sodium hypochlorite can damage cobalt-chromium; avoid it for metal frameworks. Manage denture stomatitis by addressing fit and hygiene as well as candidal infection.

Xerostomia can impair retention and increase caries and candidosis. When cleaning or attendance will be unreliable, favour hygienic, maintainable designs rather than elaborate fixed work that the patient cannot support.

Miconazole interacts with warfarin; check current prescribing advice. Deep caries may favour selective caries removal rather than complete removal over the pulp. Do not assume every missing tooth needs replacement.

CORE PRINCIPLE

Most materials questions are clinical-choice problems: match properties, preparation, substrate and maintenance.

01 Bonding must match the substrate

Silica-based glass ceramics such as lithium disilicate require a suitable hydrofluoric-acid/silane-based protocol according to manufacturer directions. Zirconia is not etched in the same way; the F Pros source emphasises airborne-particle abrasion and MDP chemistry.

02 Preparations and restorability

A short, over-tapered crown preparation cannot be repaired by simply using more cement. Consider height, resistance form and an adhesive or auxiliary feature. A post retains a core; it does not strengthen a root-filled tooth.

03 What failures reveal

Layered ceramics are more vulnerable to chipping in some parafunctional settings; monolithic options may reduce the veneer fracture mode. Soft-tissue inflammation may indicate an overcontoured or deep subgingival margin rather than a defective ceramic itself.

04 Cost-benefit and consent

Compare repairability, retrievability, opposing-tooth wear, tooth reduction, aesthetics, laboratory workflow and replacement cost. Patient preference matters only after the clinical contraindications and maintenance requirements are explained.

05 Exam comparison

E-modulus tells you about stiffness, not toughness. Do not confuse flexural strength with wear behaviour, or high strength with a good bond. Treat the manufacturer-specific protocols as decisive.

SCOPE / GUIDANCE

Select treatment within competence. Do not convert laboratory handling principles into unsupervised specialist procedures.

CORE PRINCIPLE

Select investigations only when their results may change the diagnosis, prognosis or plan.

01 History → examination → hypothesis

Start with the patient’s concern, medical and dental history, risk factors and expectations. Examine soft tissue, hard tissue, periodontal support, denture-bearing tissues, tooth wear, function and occlusion. Generate a differential diagnosis before ordering further tests.

02 Choose the minimum useful imaging

Bitewings help with proximal caries and crestal bone; periapicals with periapical or root pathology; panoramic imaging for overview; CBCT only for an appropriate three-dimensional question that cannot be answered by lower-dose methods.

03 Interpret results together

A normal sensibility response does not prove vascular vitality, and radiographs have limitations. Periodontal attachment levels and clinical probing complement imaging. Read tests in the context of the history and other findings.

04 Record the implication

State whether a radiographic finding changes restorability, root configuration, bone support, anatomical safety or the feasible replacement options. Uncertain findings may require review, repeat tests or referral.

05 SBA traps

Do not select CBCT as routine screening; justify the field of view and radiation exposure. A normal radiograph does not overrule a concerning clinical examination or suspicious mucosal lesion.

Stabilise disease first; choose a maintainable option that fits the patient’s prognosis and priorities.

01 The five-phase plan

Urgent relief of pain or infection → stabilisation of periodontal/caries risk → re-evaluation → definitive restoration or replacement → supportive maintenance. A plan that starts with definitive crowns before disease control is usually unsound.

CORE PRINCIPLE

Start from the saddle and support; only then choose clasps, connector and indirect retention.

01 Design in order

Saddles → support → retention → bracing/reciprocation → major connectors → indirect retention → review. Identify Kennedy class after extractions; a distal-extension RPD needs tooth-and-mucosa support.

02 Biomechanical consequences

Mesial rests and stress-releasing concepts may reduce torque on distal-extension abutments. Survey casts, establish the insertion path and create suitable guide planes and rest seats before taking the definitive impression.

03 Numbers worth interpreting

The F Pros source gives a common 0.25 mm Co-Cr undercut and approximately 0.5 mm for cast gold/wrought wire. A lingual bar generally needs adequate functional sulcus depth, approximately 7–8 mm between the gingival margin and floor of mouth; otherwise consider a lingual plate.

04 Common failures

Pain after fitting may arise from overextension, tissue pressure or a high rest seat. Distal-extension saddles lose support as the ridge changes; assess the need for reline rather than merely tightening a clasp.

05 Exam trap

A retentive clasp without effective reciprocation is not a complete design. Choosing a clasp before assessing support and insertion path reverses the design logic.

SCOPE / GUIDANCE

Framework design must be individualized to teeth, anatomy and support; numerical rules are approximate.

Displace flabby tissue as little as clinically appropriate; selective pressure or a window technique may help. Functional borders and the posterior palatal seal have to be assessed clinically.

03 Occlusal scheme and OVD

CORE PRINCIPLE

A sound answer diagnoses why a prosthesis failed before choosing the repair or remake.

01 Diagnose by category

GOVERNANCE AND COMMUNICATION

CORE PRINCIPLE

The Diploma tests the independent judgement of an enhanced-skills practitioner, not specialist surgery. Defensible care includes knowing when to refer.

Uncontrolled disease before extensive restorationDefinitive prosthesis is premature Stabilise caries, periodontitis and risk factors first
Complex rehabilitation, advanced perio/implant surgeryOutcome beyond operator competenceRefer with diagnostic records and clear clinical question

CLINICAL DISCRIMINATOR

Consent requires an individualised explanation of likely benefit, risks, uncertainty, maintenance, complications, alternatives and the option of no replacement. Record decision capacity, patient priorities and how risks were communicated. Relevant audit outcomes include prosthesis survival, patient-reported function, recalls, repair incidence and clinical stability.

CASE CONTRAST / NEAR MISS

A patient asks for multiple implants despite active Stage IV periodontitis and poor attendance. A specialist referral and disease-control sequence is more defensible than immediate implant surgery even if the procedure is technically possible.

EXAM TRAP

Passing an examination does not replace local credentialing or establish competence to undertake every Level 2 procedure.

DIPLOMA LEARNING OUTCOMES Across 1, 2a, 2b, 2c and 3a–3b

CORE PRINCIPLE

Use these prompts to test the decisions common to both source notebooks—not as official examination predictions.

01 Diagnose before intervening

A suspicious lesion needs investigation/referral, not camouflage by a prosthesis. In ambiguous test results, repeat or complement the clinical examination instead of forcing a diagnosis.

02 Stabilise and preserve

Caries/periodontal control precedes complex definitive work. Prefer additive or reversible approaches where they meet the need; no treatment is a legitimate comparator for a missing tooth.

03 Design with forces in mind

RPD support precedes clasp selection; a distal extension needs indirect retention and maintenance. Low denture retention can be functional-border failure rather than an adhesive deficiency.

04 Material chemistry matters

A post retains, not reinforces. Silane is for suitable silica-based surfaces, not zirconia. Good cement cannot rescue a short and severely over-tapered preparation.

05 Recognise your level

Routine Level 2 planning may include moderately difficult tooth replacement; complex multidisciplinary care, severe parafunction, full-mouth reorganisation, complex implants and specialist trauma management need escalation.

06 Answer the clinical question asked

Look for “first,” “most appropriate” or “least invasive,” and select the patient-safe next action rather than the most sophisticated possible procedure.

SCOPE / GUIDANCE

Revision prompts are educational summaries of the supplied notes; exact standards and medications require current verification.
